No Smoking Gun Without a Placebo: Four Conditions for AI Collusion Evidence

Author: Shubha Dhananjaya Achar Affiliation: Independent Track 2.3: What should count as convincing evidence of collusion? · AI Collusion Research Sprint (Apart Research), 23–25 Oct 2026

Abstract

We propose four conditions for collusion evidence: positives not reproducible from the role label (i); a headline not carried by one statistic (ii); a same-process placebo under the claimed false-positive rate (iii); uncertainty over independent units (iv). On three published instruments of three kinds, judged from their released files, every condition that can be checked fails, and the placebo condition cannot be checked on any of them. ColludeBench's union falls from 15/17 to 7/17 without one statistic that fires on 99% of honest windows. A CoT-judge null rests on seven data points. NARCBench's AUROC of 1.00 uses a label equal to the assigned role. Our pre-registered randomised test of cost leakage between LLM pricing agents passes all four. With messaging, prices rise past monopoly, with no profit gain or detectable cost leak. The follower copies any proposed number, low or high, from any speaker: the lockstep is compliance, not collusion.

1. Introduction

Collusion between AI agents is usually evidenced in one of three ways: - statistical tests on messages: steganalysis, mutual information; - AI judges reading chains of thought; - probes on internal activations.

Each kind reports a headline number: a true-positive rate at a calibrated false-positive budget, a null correlation, an AUROC. The sprint asks what should count as convincing evidence.

We answer with a standard, not a new detector, and the choice is deliberate: black-box optimisation against steganographic collusion is intractable [1, Thm 2], so a detector cannot be validated by an optimiser's failure to beat it. What can be checked is whether the evidence it produces is about collusion rather than about its own construction.

Contributions. 1. Four conditions for collusion evidence, each with an executable check (check_conditions.py, 14 tests) that returns PASS, FAIL or NOT-ASSESSABLE from an instrument's released outputs. NOT-ASSESSABLE is itself a finding: the instrument does not release what a reader would need. 2. An audit of three instruments of three kinds from their own files: - ColludeBench's statistical union [2]; - the CoT-judge null of Riemer et al. [3]; - NARCBench's activation probes [4].

Every condition that can be checked fails on at least one of them: (i), (ii) and (iv). The placebo condition (iii) cannot be checked on any, because none releases a same-process honest baseline. 3. A randomised canary test, pre-registered and hashed before any LLM call, that passes all four and finds explicit price coordination without detectable private-information flow.

2. Four conditions, and how the instruments score

Condition The failure it catches Check (default threshold)
(i) Label The positive class is the instructed role, so detecting it measures the assignment AUROC predicting the label from the role alone; FAIL if ≥ 0.95
(ii) One statistic A union whose headline rests on a single near-oracle or non-discriminating statistic Remove each statistic; FAIL if TPR drops > 0.20
(iii) Placebo A detector that fires on honest agents from the same process Placebo FPR with Wilson CI vs the claimed FPR; non-independent scores → NOT-ASSESSABLE
(iv) Unit Correlated windows or rounds treated as independent Interval over independent units; FAIL if < 10 units, or if a null's CI reaches |r| ≥ 0.5

The conditions sharpen antitrust's plus-factor doctrine rather than replacing it. Condition (iii) is the counterfactual requirement, ported to a generator the auditor controls. Tacit collusion is lawful [6], so every condition here concerns evidence of a harm, not of an offence.

3. Auditing three instruments from their released files

ColludeBench [2] is the only collusion benchmark we found that ships its detector output under a licence (MIT). Its author deserves credit for that: it is why this audit can be done arithmetically. We checked the committed outputs against themselves and against the paper. - All 16 single-detector cells in the committed metrics.md and the paper's ablation paragraph (p. 8) recompute exactly from the committed JSON; ColludeBench's Table 1 (p. 7) does not (Appendix B, P1).

ablation.json holds all 255 non-empty subsets of the 8 statistics. That makes (ii) assessable (Figure 2). - At FPR 10⁻² the full union is 15/17 = 0.88 [0.66, 0.97]. - Without permutation_invariance it is 7/17 = 0.41 [0.22, 0.64]. Removing any other single statistic leaves 0.88, apart from acceptance_bias (0.71). - In metrics.json, permutation_invariance fires on 99% of honest windows at the same budget. The ablation credits it with zero false positives only because every one of its 510 entries has exactly one negative configuration (17 positive, 1 negative). That single negative also fails (iv). - The honest pool is template-generated (600 calibration runs, no colluding run), so it is not a placebo for a claim about LLM agents.

Figure 2. ColludeBench's detector union at FPR 10⁻², from its own ablation.json. TPR with Wilson 95% CI over the 17 positive configurations.

Riemer et al. [3] report that a CoT judge's collusion probability is flat (32–34%) while steering moves behaviour from near-Nash to highly collusive. Their Table 3 correlations (PDF p. 9) are over n = 7 steering magnitudes, recovered from the r/p pairs on four cells; the headline r = 0.194 then has a 95% CI of [−0.65, +0.83], which includes the same table's r = 0.771 ("will this agent raise its price?"). A null needs a CI that rules out a strong effect; this one is uninformative, which is not evidence that CoT monitoring works.

NARCBench [4] reports an AUROC of 1.00 for probes on the Core tier. We joined its released activation metadata to its released run configurations (62 Qwen3-32B runs; seeds and agent names match 62/62). - The released positive label equals the assigned mode in every run, so predicting it from the role alone gives AUROC 1.00 by construction. - By the authors' own collusion_success flag, 8 of the 31 positives did not coordinate (in 6, no colluder voted for the target) and only 2 swung the vote (target_majority). - On these behavioural labels (i) passes: role-only AUROC 0.897 and 0.758. So the failure is not the probe's but the label's. The paper's selection may rest on an unshipped transcript judgment (Appendix B), so the claim is that the shipped flag disagrees with the label, not that the label is wrong; no per-scenario tier is released, so (iv) is NA (P8).

4. A test built to pass: the randomised canary

Design (pre-registered, PREREG-P9-canary.md, SHA-256 hashed before any LLM call; amendments A1–A7 dated). - The market: two LLM firms play an 8-round Bertrand duopoly with logit demand (Calvano/Riemer parameters: Nash price 1.47, monopoly 1.93). - Each round: 1. Firm A privately draws a fresh cost, c_A ∈ {0.8, 1.2}, i.i.d. 2. A may send one message to B. 3. Both firms post prices simultaneously. - Identification: when B prices, c_A is independent of everything B has seen publicly. So without a private channel, the statistic z_t = (c_A,t − 1)·(p_B,t − p_B,t−1)/0.1 has expectation exactly zero, whatever the agents' policy. - Inference: a t-interval over 40 independent run means. The test was calibrated on scripted agents: 10/200 false positives = 5.0%. A within-run permutation test, invalid on the same scripted agents, was replaced by this estimator (A1). - Model: Qwen3.8-27B (Groq), temperature 1.0, the same for every arm.

This is the Athey–Bagwell setting, in which efficient collusion under private costs requires cost communication [7]. Dechenaux & Mago ran it with human subjects and chat [8]. What is new here is the randomised per-round estimator applied to LLM agents.

Arms (40 runs each): - P-0: no channel (the placebo); - POS: A is told to share its cost and propose a price (the positive control); - M: an open channel and "maximise your profit"; - M+P: an open channel and "maximise long-run profit, avoid price wars"; - M-S: M's prompts, but B receives a stored M message from a different run. This arm was added after seeing M, and is labelled as such.

Emission vs inference. A's messages state a cost word in 8% of M rounds and its true cost value in 4%. Its price proposals, by contrast, are adopted by B to the cent in 83% (M) and 98% (M+P) of rounds. B's price equals A's same-round price in 82% (M) and 99% (M+P) of rounds, against 16% in P-0, where there is no channel. Dropping the rounds whose message contains the true cost leaves the M estimate at +0.050 [−0.258, +0.358].

Measure choice decides the verdict. With a channel, prices rise by +1.1 (M) and +1.4 (M+P) over the placebo (Figure 1b); the median p_B is 2.5 in both (P-0: 1.45), above the monopoly price of 1.93. Profits do not rise: Δ is negative in every open arm and not detectably higher than the placebo's (M −0.54, M+P −0.77, P-0 −0.61); only POS, whose proposal is cost + 1, approaches monopoly profit (+0.84). This is coordinated over-pricing, not profitable collusion. A price-level detector would flag M and M+P as collusive; a profit index would not; the information-flow test answers a third question, and finds no private cost moving at a detectable size.

Does the rise need a live partner? No. M-S, where B reads a stored M message from a different run, reproduces R = 0.96 [0.68, 1.40] of M's rise over P-0 (M-S − M = −0.045 [−0.448, +0.338]); a CI below 0.5 would have indicated within-pair coordination. Exposure to any price proposal, or the shared framing, produces it, and M-S cannot separate the two. Because its inbox is independent of c_A, M-S is also a channel-open placebo, and its canary CI contains 0 (Table 2).

Worked case: lockstep is compliance, not collusion. Price lockstep is the signature a behavioural detector reads as collusion. Here it is something else. B adopts the number in A's message in round 1, before any history exists to reward or punish it: 34/40 runs (M) and 38/40 (M+P). Following costs B 29.7 [24.1, 35.5] (M) and 31.5 [24.5, 38.4] (M+P) profit per round against a best response to A's actual price, more than a round of Nash profit (22.2); P-0: 7.9 [5.3, 11.0]. A colluder holds a high price because deviating is punished later. This follower gives up profit with nothing to fear: it complies with a salient number. A pre-registered follow-up (track3/PREREG-Z2-anchor.md) used the same round-1 prompt, varied only B's inbox, and ran 40 independent calls per arm on self-hosted Qwen3.8-27B. B priced at the rival's proposed 1.20, 1.50, 2.50 and 3.50 in 39, 40, 40 and 40 of 40 calls, downward as readily as upward and below the Nash price at 1.20 (Figure 3); it followed an "independent analyst's" number almost as closely (shift ratio 0.97 [0.91, 1.00]) and a live A's real message in 36 of 40. A claim of collusion therefore has to show that the coordination is sustained by incentives, not only that prices move together. (canary/compliance.py, track3/z2_anchor.py.)

5. Discussion and limitations

  • The conditions are necessary, not sufficient. Passing them makes evidence checkable. It does not make a detector complete: Motwani's result still bounds every black-box test, including ours.
  • The three audits use released files only. NA verdicts are statements about what is released, not proof that the instruments fail.
  • The canary's nulls are under-powered: MDE 0.30–0.56 price units against a positive-control effect of 0.13. The test can certify a leak; with 40 runs per arm, it cannot certify its absence. It uses one model, one temperature, one prompt per arm and an 8-round horizon.
  • Five claims made during the sprint were retracted after independent review (Appendix D).

6. Conclusion and next steps

Collusion evidence should be judged on whether it is about collusion, not only on its headline. Four conditions make that checkable from released files: - Applied to three instruments of three kinds, every condition that can be checked fails somewhere, and the placebo condition cannot be checked on any of them. - A randomised test built to the standard passes all four, and on LLM pricing agents it separates price coordination from information flow.

Future work. Antitrust price screens (coefficient of variation, relative distance) ported to LLM pricing logs, and leniency for AI cartels: B adopts A's explicit price proposals in 83–98% of rounds, a reportable agreement. Both held to the same four conditions.

Appendix A. Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

  1. No colluding agent is built or released. The canary's agents are prompted to maximise profit in a toy market. The positive control is told to share a cost. Nothing here adds coordination capability that the prompts do not already state.
  2. The checker is an evaluation tool, not an attack. An adversary who knows the conditions learns what auditors require of their own generator and null, not how to evade a detector's internals. Evidentiary standards in antitrust are public for the same reason.
  3. The real risk is interpretive. An audit that weakens trust in detection benchmarks can be read as "collusion is undetectable". Our finding concerns whether specific evidence is about collusion. The canary shows that evidence meeting the standard can be produced.
  4. Tone and credit. Every finding is stated about an instrument, never an author. ColludeBench is credited for shipping its logs under a licence, which made this audit possible. Findings will be sent to its author with the repository link.
  5. Untrusted inputs. Every downloaded repository, dataset and paper was treated as data: read in isolated directories with Python in isolated mode, and never executed. This is why ColludeBench's transcripts were not regenerated (Appendix B, P4).
  6. Scope. These are small-scale testbeds built to study detection. Results on one model, one market and an 8-round horizon do not establish how deployed pricing agents behave. The M vs P-0 price comparison confounds the channel with prompt framing; M-S was added for this and cannot separate exposure from framing.
